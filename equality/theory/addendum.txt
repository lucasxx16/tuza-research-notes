Claim A1 (Cartesian additivity). Let F and H be finite simple graphs.
For the Cartesian product P = F square H,

nu(P) = |V(H)| nu(F) + |V(F)| nu(H),
tau(P) = |V(H)| tau(F) + |V(F)| tau(H).

Definitions. V(P) = V(F) x V(H). Vertices (a,h),(b,k) are adjacent
if h=k and ab is an F-edge, or if a=b and hk is an H-edge. A triangle
packing is a set of triangles sharing no edge; nu is its maximum size.
A triangle transversal is a set of edges meeting every triangle; tau
is its minimum size. For each h in V(H) there is an F-fiber
V(F) x {h}, and for each a in V(F) there is an H-fiber {a} x V(H).

Proof. A product edge changes exactly one coordinate. In a triangle,
if both coordinate types occur, one coordinate type occurs exactly
once. Traversing that edge changes its coordinate to a different value,
and traversing the other two edges cannot restore that coordinate.
This contradicts closure of the triangle. Hence all three edges change
the same coordinate, so every triangle lies in a factor fiber.

The edge sets of all F-fibers and H-fibers partition E(P): the unchanged
coordinate and its value determine the unique fiber of any edge.
Distinct fibers may share vertices, but never edges. Every triangle is
therefore in exactly one fiber. A packing restricts to a packing in each
fiber, bounding its size by the sum of the fiber packing maxima. Taking
maximum packings in every fiber attains this sum, since the fiber edge
sets are disjoint. This proves the formula for nu.

A transversal must, within each fiber's edge set, meet every triangle
of that fiber. Its size is therefore at least the sum of the fiber
transversal minima. The union of minimum transversals in all the fibers
meets every triangle of P and has precisely that size. This proves the
formula for tau. End of proof.

Claim A2. nu(K_4)=1, tau(K_4)=2, nu(K_5)=2, and tau(K_5)=4.

Proof for K_4. There is a triangle, and any two distinct triangles in
four vertices share an edge. Thus nu(K_4)=1. Deleting one edge ab does
not remove the triangle on a and the other two vertices, so a cover
needs at least two edges. Deleting a perfect matching leaves a 4-cycle,
so two suffice. Hence tau(K_4)=2.

Proof for the packing number of K_5. Label its vertices 1 through 5.
The triangles 123 and 145 are edge-disjoint, so nu(K_5)>=2. A packing
has at most floor(10/3)=3 triangles by the edge count. If there were
three, their nine edges would leave exactly one unused edge. At every
vertex, the packing uses twice the number of triangles through that
vertex, an even number of incident edges. Since the original degree is
four, the unused-edge graph would have even degree at every vertex.
A graph with exactly one edge has two odd degrees, which is impossible.
Thus nu(K_5)=2.

Proof for the transversal number of K_5. Deleting 12,34,35,45 leaves
the complete bipartite graph with parts {1,2} and {3,4,5}, which is
triangle-free; hence tau(K_5)<=4. To prove the reverse inequality, we
show directly that a triangle-free graph J on five vertices has at most
six edges. If its maximum degree is at most two, it has at most five
edges by summing degrees. Otherwise, take a maximum-degree vertex v
of degree d, where d is three or four. Its neighbors are independent,
since J is triangle-free. If d=4, all four other vertices are neighbors
of v and there are no edges between them, so J has four edges. If d=3,
let x be the only vertex other than v and its three neighbors. The edge
vx is absent because v has degree three, and the three neighbors of v
have no mutual edges. Besides the three edges at v, all edges must
therefore run from x to those three neighbors. This gives at most six
edges in total. The asserted bound follows in all cases. Removing a
transversal from K_5 consequently leaves at most six of its ten edges,
so at least four edges were removed. Thus tau(K_5)=4. End of proof.

Claim A3 (D's construction). There is a finite simple connected
7-regular graph G on 20 vertices with nu(G)=13 and tau(G)=26.
Every edge of G belongs to a triangle.

Proof. Set
V(G) = {1,2,3,4} x {1,2,3,4,5},
and join two distinct vertices if they have the same first coordinate
or the same second coordinate. Thus G = K_4 square K_5. For every
fixed first coordinate i, the five vertices (i,1),...,(i,5) form a
K_5 row. For every fixed second coordinate j, the four vertices
(1,j),...,(4,j) form a K_4 column.

The explicit adjacency relation gives a finite simple graph. A vertex
has four other vertices in its row and three other vertices in its
column as neighbors; these sets are disjoint. Thus G is 7-regular.
The path (i,j),(i,j'),(i',j'), with consecutive repetitions omitted,
joins any (i,j) to any (i',j'). Hence G is connected. Its order is
20 and its size is 20 times 7 / 2 = 70.

Claim A1 and Claim A2 give the exact values
nu(G) = 5 nu(K_4) + 4 nu(K_5) = 5 + 8 = 13,
tau(G) = 5 tau(K_4) + 4 tau(K_5) = 10 + 16 = 26.
In particular nu(G)>0 and tau(G)=2 nu(G).

For explicit optimal witnesses, in each column j take the triangle
{(1,j),(2,j),(3,j)}, and in each row i take the triangles
{(i,1),(i,2),(i,3)} and {(i,1),(i,4),(i,5)}. These are thirteen
pairwise edge-disjoint triangles, because the chosen triangles within
each fiber are edge-disjoint, and different fibers have disjoint edge
sets. For a transversal, in each column j take
(1,j)(2,j) and (3,j)(4,j),
and in each row i take
(i,1)(i,2), (i,3)(i,4), (i,3)(i,5), (i,4)(i,5).
This gives twenty-six distinct edges. It meets every triangle within
each fiber by the local calculations in Claim A2, and all triangles
belong to fibers by Claim A1. The exact formulas above prove that the
packing and transversal are optimal.

Finally, every edge belongs to one K_4 or K_5 fiber and lies in a
triangle of that complete graph. End of proof.

REVIEW CONCLUSION

The general formula in Claim A1 is valid without any regularity or
triangle-free hypothesis on the factors. In particular, whenever both
factors satisfy tau=2 nu, so does their Cartesian product. Factors may
have nu=0; the resulting product has positive nu whenever at least one
nonempty factor has positive nu and the other has a vertex. The initial
E example and the independent D example are both instances.

D's submitted proof is sound. In particular, its two key lower/upper
optimality arguments for K_5 are valid: the one-edge leftover would
violate parity, and every triangle-free graph on five vertices has at
most six edges. Intersections of rows and columns occur only in single
vertices and cannot create a shared edge or a mixed triangle.

There are no unresolved proof gaps in the selected existence result.
No computational optimization or exhaustive search is required. Optional
corroboration for this 20-vertex graph can enumerate 70 edges and
binomial(20,3)=1140 vertex triples: exactly 5 times 4 + 4 times 10 = 60
triples should be triangles, and the displayed packing and transversal
can be checked directly. Computation cannot replace the elementary
optimality bounds, which are already proved above.

No novelty, minimum-order, uniqueness, or full equality-characterization
claim is made.
