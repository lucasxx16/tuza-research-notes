A connected 7-regular graph with positive nu and tau = 2 nu
Researcher E, round 1

RESULT

There exists a finite simple connected 7-regular graph G on 32 vertices
such that nu(G) = 8 and tau(G) = 16. In particular, the 7-regular
existence subquestion following Question 12.3 of the source paper has an
affirmative answer, with positive triangle packing number. The example is
G = K_4 square K_{4,4}, where square denotes the Cartesian graph product.
There are also arbitrarily large connected 7-regular examples.

DEFINITIONS AND CONSTRUCTION

All graphs here are finite, simple and undirected. A triangle is a set of
three distinct vertices with all three edges present. A triangle packing
is a set of pairwise edge-disjoint triangles, and nu(G) is its maximum
size. A triangle transversal is a set of edges meeting every triangle,
and tau(G) is its minimum size.

For graphs F and H, the Cartesian product F square H has vertex set
V(F) x V(H). Distinct vertices (a,h) and (b,k) are adjacent precisely if
one of the following holds:
  (i) h = k and ab is an edge of F;
  (ii) a = b and hk is an edge of H.
Thus an edge changes exactly one coordinate, along an edge of its factor.

Let I = {1,2,3,4}, and let H have disjoint vertex sets
L = {l_1,l_2,l_3,l_4} and R = {r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4}, with an edge l_j r_k
for every j,k in I and no other edges. Thus H = K_{4,4}. Define
G = K_4 square H, where V(K_4) = I. Explicitly, its 32 vertices are
(i,l_j) and (i,r_j), for i,j in I. Its edges are exactly:
  (a) (i,h)(i',h) for every h in L union R and distinct i,i' in I;
  (b) (i,l_j)(i,r_k) for every i,j,k in I.
No implicit identifications, multiple edges, or further edges are used.

Claim 1. G is finite, simple, connected, and 7-regular. It has 32
vertices and 112 edges.

Proof. The displayed vertex and edge sets define a finite simple graph.
For any vertex (i,h), there are three neighbors (i',h) with i' different
from i. There are also four neighbors (i,k), one for each H-neighbor k
of h. These seven neighbors are distinct, so every degree is seven.
The order is 4 times 8 = 32, and summing degrees gives
|E(G)| = 32 times 7 / 2 = 112.

The graph H is connected: two vertices in opposite parts are adjacent,
and two distinct vertices in the same part have a common neighbor in the
other nonempty part. Given (i,h) and (j,k), first follow an H-path from
h to k while keeping the first coordinate i fixed. If i differs from j,
then take the edge from (i,k) to (j,k). This gives a G-path between any
two vertices, proving connectedness. End of proof.

Claim 2. Every triangle of a Cartesian product F square H lies in a
copy of one factor with its other coordinate fixed. In particular, if
H is triangle-free, every triangle of K_4 square H lies inside
Q_h = I x {h} for some h in V(H).

Proof. Each of the three edges of a triangle changes exactly one
coordinate. If both coordinate types occur among the three edges, then
one type occurs exactly once. Traverse the closed walk around the
triangle, starting just before that unique edge. In its coordinate the
unique edge changes the value to a different value, and the other two
edges do not change that coordinate. The walk therefore cannot return
to its starting vertex. This is a contradiction. Consequently all three
edges change the same coordinate, and the other coordinate is fixed.
The three values in the changing coordinate form a triangle in the
corresponding factor.

The graph H = K_{4,4} is triangle-free, because each edge switches its
bipartition class, and three such switches cannot return to the starting
class. Thus, in our G, all triangles lie inside some Q_h. Each induced
graph G[Q_h] is a copy of K_4 by the definition of the product. End of
proof.

Claim 3. A copy of K_4 has triangle packing number one and triangle
transversal number two.

Proof. It contains a triangle. Any two different triangles in its four
vertices have exactly two vertices in common, and hence share the edge
on those vertices. Thus a packing has at most one triangle, and its
maximum size is one.

No single edge is a transversal: if the removed edge is ab, and c,d
are the other two vertices, then acd is still a triangle. The empty set
also fails to meet a triangle. Conversely, the two edges of any perfect
matching meet every triangle. Indeed, a three-element subset of a set
partitioned into two pairs contains both elements of at least one pair.
Equivalently, deleting that matching leaves a 4-cycle. Hence the minimum
transversal size is two. End of proof.

Claim 4. For G constructed above, nu(G) = 8.

Proof. There are eight fibers Q_h. By Claim 2, every triangle in a
packing belongs to one of them. By Claim 3, a packing contains at most
one triangle from each fiber, giving nu(G) <= 8. Conversely, take the
triangle with vertices (1,h),(2,h),(3,h) for each of the eight values
of h. These eight triangles have disjoint vertex sets and therefore
disjoint edge sets. They form a packing of size eight, proving equality.
End of proof.

Claim 5. For G constructed above, tau(G) = 16.

Proof. Let X be any triangle transversal of G. For each h, every
triangle in Q_h uses only edges inside Q_h. Thus
X intersect E(G[Q_h]) must itself meet all triangles of that K_4.
Claim 3 forces at least two edges in this intersection. The eight
fiber edge sets are pairwise disjoint, so |X| >= 8 times 2 = 16.
Edges outside the fibers cannot meet a triangle inside a fiber.

For an explicit transversal attaining this bound, take
X_0 = union over h in V(H) of
      { (1,h)(2,h), (3,h)(4,h) }.
It has sixteen edges. In each fiber it is a perfect matching, so it
meets all triangles there by Claim 3. Claim 2 says these are all the
triangles of G, so X_0 is a transversal. This proves tau(G) = 16.
End of proof.

The five claims establish the required conclusion:
G is finite, simple, connected and 7-regular, and
tau(G) = 16 = 2 times 8 = 2 nu(G), with nu(G) > 0.

GENERAL FAMILY

Claim 6. If H is any nonempty connected triangle-free d-regular graph,
then K_4 square H is connected and (d+3)-regular, and
nu(K_4 square H) = |V(H)|,
tau(K_4 square H) = 2 |V(H)|.

Proof. The degree and connectedness arguments in Claim 1 apply with d
in place of four. Claim 2 places every triangle in a K_4 fiber, and the
arguments in Claims 4 and 5 apply verbatim, with |V(H)| fibers instead
of eight. The value |V(H)| is positive by the nonempty hypothesis.
End of proof.

For a fully explicit unbounded family at degree seven, let m be any
integer at least four and define H_m on the residue classes modulo 2m.
Join t to t+1, t-1, t+3 and t-3, with arithmetic modulo 2m. None of
these differences is zero, and the four differences are distinct modulo
2m, since 2m >= 8. Thus H_m is simple and 4-regular. Every difference
is odd, so the even and odd residue classes form a bipartition; in
particular H_m is triangle-free. The edges t(t+1) already form a spanning
cycle, so H_m is connected. Claim 6 now gives

G_m = K_4 square H_m,
|V(G_m)| = 8m, |E(G_m)| = 28m,
nu(G_m) = 2m, tau(G_m) = 4m,

and every G_m is simple, connected and 7-regular. At m = 4, every even
residue is adjacent to all four odd residues, so H_4 = K_{4,4} and G_4
is the 32-vertex example above.

SCOPE AND VERIFICATION

This proves the existence subquestion, not a characterization of all
graphs attaining tau = 2 nu. No minimal-order assertion is made. No
result from the source paper, no implementation of an optimization
solver, and no exhaustive census is used in any proof above.

Optional finite sanity check for the displayed 32-vertex example:
enumerate its 32 vertices, 112 edges and all binomial(32,3) = 4960
vertex triples. Exactly 32 triples should be triangles, four in each
fiber; the eight listed packing triangles and sixteen listed transversal
edges should satisfy their definitions. Such a check is corroborative
only and is not needed to establish optimality or the theorem.
