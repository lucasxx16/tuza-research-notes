ROUND 6 / D
A PRESCRIBED TRIANGLE EDGE CAN BE COVERED WITHIN TUZA'S BOUND WHEN nu<=2

Status: reviewed research proof; see C/review_D_full.txt.
No novelty claim is made. The proof is self-contained and uses no
computation or assumption of Tuza's conjecture on other graphs.

THEOREM.
Let G be a finite simple graph with nu(G) in {1,2}, where nu denotes
maximum edge-disjoint triangle packing size. For every edge e that
belongs to a triangle of G, there exists a triangle edge cover X with
  e in X and |X|<=2nu(G).

Consequently, if nu(G)=2 and tau(G)=4, every edge belonging to a
triangle belongs to SOME minimum triangle cover. The minimum cover
may depend on the edge. An edge in no triangle is not covered by the
claim about prescribed edges.

1. THE PACKING-NUMBER-ONE STRUCTURE.

LEMMA.
If nu(J)=1, either all triangles of J share a common edge, or all
triangles of J are precisely the four triangles of one K4. In either
case, for any edge e that belongs to a triangle of J, there is a cover
of J of size at most two containing e.

Proof. Any two triangles share an edge, since otherwise they form an
edge-disjoint packing of size two. If every triangle has one edge g
in common, {g,e} is the required cover (or {e} if g=e).

Otherwise choose distinct triangles abc and abd, sharing ab. Some
triangle T avoids ab, since ab is not common to all triangles. It must
share an edge with each of abc and abd. An edge of {ac,bc} and an edge
of {ad,bd} can lie in one triangle only when they meet; the resulting
triangle is acd or bcd. Thus T is one of these two, and all six edges
on {a,b,c,d} are present. They induce a K4.

Any other triangle must share an edge with every one of this K4's four
triangles. One K4 edge belongs to only two of those four triangles,
so it must contain at least two distinct K4 edges. Those edges meet
within the triangle and force all three of its vertices into the K4.
Therefore all triangles lie in this K4. Given e, take it and its
opposite edge in the K4. These two edges form a perfect matching and
meet all four K4 triangles, proving the lemma.

In particular every graph with nu<=1 has tau<=2. If such a graph has
tau=2, its entire triangle family lies in one K4; the common-edge case
would instead have tau=1. Edges outside the K4 may exist but belong
to no triangle.

2. PROOF FOR PACKING NUMBER TWO.

Fix nu(G)=2 and an edge e belonging to a triangle T. Delete all three
edges of T and put G0=G-E(T). Then nu(G0)<=1: two edge-disjoint
triangles of G0, together with T, would pack three in G. By Section 1,
tau(G0)<=2.

If tau(G0)<=1, choose a cover Y of G0 of size at most one. The set
E(T) union Y covers G, has size at most four, and contains e. Indeed,
every triangle meeting E(T) is covered there, and every other triangle
survives in G0 and meets Y. This settles the case.

Suppose now tau(G0)=2. Section 1 gives a four-vertex set C such that
G0[C]=K4 and every triangle of G0 lies in C. Write K for this K4 and
E(K) for its six edges. Its edges are disjoint from E(T), because K
lies in G0. Consequently
  |C intersect V(T)|<=1:
two common vertices would have an edge belonging to both K and T.

Every vertex outside C has at most one neighbor in C in G0. If a vertex
had two, their K4 edge would complete a triangle of G0 not contained
in C, contradicting the description of G0's triangle family.

Delete the K4 edges instead and put F=G-E(K). The triangle T survives
in F, so nu(F)>=1. If F had two edge-disjoint triangles, adding any
triangle of K would produce three edge-disjoint triangles in G, since
F contains no edge of K. Hence nu(F)=1. By Section 1, F has a cover
Q with e in Q and |Q|<=2.

Triangles entirely in C can be covered by a two-edge perfect matching
of K. Triangles using no edge of K lie in F and are covered by Q.
We next show that the remaining, mixed triangles can all be met by
one suitably chosen edge of K.

A mixed triangle is a triangle of G that uses an edge of K but is not
wholly contained in C. It must use an edge of T: otherwise it would
be a triangle of G0 outside C, which is impossible.

If C and V(T) are disjoint, a mixed triangle would need two vertices
in C to use a K-edge and two vertices outside C to use a T-edge.
A triangle has only three vertices, so there are no mixed triangles.

Otherwise write C intersect V(T)={w} and T=wab. A mixed triangle
must be of the form wap or wbq, where p,q belong to C\{w}. For
example, a triangle using a K-edge has two C-vertices and only one
vertex outside C; to contain a T-edge that edge must be wa or wb.
The bound on C-neighbors in G0 shows that there is at most one p
yielding wap and at most one q yielding wbq: edges from a or b to
C\{w} were not deleted with T and remain in G0.

If both mixed triangles exist with p distinct from q, they are
edge-disjoint: their edges are {wa,ap,wp} and {wb,bq,wq}, with a,b
outside C and distinct p,q inside C. The triangle on the three
vertices C\{w} avoids both sets of edges. Its K-edges do not use w,
and the other edges of the two mixed triangles use a or b. These are
three edge-disjoint triangles in G, contradicting nu(G)=2.

Thus all mixed triangles, if there are any, use the same K-edge g:
either there is only one mixed triangle, or their third vertices agree
and g=wp=wq. Choose a two-edge perfect matching P of K containing g.
If there are no mixed triangles, choose any perfect matching of K.
Then P covers every triangle entirely in C and every mixed triangle.

The union X=P union Q therefore covers all triangles of G. Its size
is at most 2+2=4, and it contains e because e belongs to Q. This
proves the theorem for nu=2; Section 1 already proves nu=1.

3. THE RICH THREE-EDGE PROFILE AT k=2 IS IMPOSSIBLE.

The reviewed round-five profile is defined by a graph H and a
three-edge set D, with H(J)=H-(D\J) for J subset D. A rich side has
  tau(H)=2nu(H)=2k,
  nu(H(empty))=k-1,
  nu(H(J))=k for every nonempty J subset D,
and every edge of D belongs to no minimum cover of H. Its exact cover
profile is recorded in mad8/round5/D/equality_boundary_profiles.txt.

Each edge d in D belongs to a triangle: restoring d alone increases
the packing number from k-1 to k, which would be impossible if no
triangle used d. If k=2, the present theorem supplies a cover of H
containing d with size at most four. Since tau(H)=4, it is a minimum
cover, contradicting the required absence of d from all minimum covers.

Therefore no rich side with k=2 exists. The proof does not depend on
whether D induces P4 or K_{1,3}, and so covers both remaining sparse
four-vertex separator shapes. The same argument for nu=1 recovers the
previous k=1 exclusion. Every possible rich side must have k>=3.

This is an unconditional exclusion of the k=2 case, not just a bounded
atlas check. It does not rule out rich profiles at k>=3 and does not
complete the three-edge gluing argument in general.

SCOPE AND PROVENANCE.
The theorem and its proof use no degree bound, average-degree bound,
or connectedness assumption. They require only nu(G)<=2 and that the
prescribed edge belongs to a triangle. The consequence for rich sides
uses their established defining properties, with no added assumption
of Tuza on arbitrary subgraphs. No numerical computation or external
classification is used, and no E round-six material was inspected
before the independent draft. The full mad<8 question remains open
in this work. Research stops at this target for review.
